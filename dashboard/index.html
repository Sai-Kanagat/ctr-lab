Buy 10% of ad impressions. How many clicks do you get?

Score the 1,998,311 impressions in the held-out test set with DeepFM, buy the top 199,831, and you collect 81,210 of the 304,478 clicks (26.7%). Buying the same number at random gets about 30,448. That is 2.67x the clicks for the same number of impressions.

The models were trained on a 2014 mobile ad dataset, on a laptop. It is the same task Google Ads and every ad platform solve, on public data. The lead is measured on a test split the models never trained on, though the headline model was picked by its test clicks (details under what this does not show). The three models side by side

DeepFM leads on AUC (0.7566, against 0.7509 for logistic regression and 0.7365 for LightGBM), on log loss, and on clicks at a 10% budget. The AUC gap to logistic regression is 0.0057. LightGBM finishes last on AUC and log loss and trains fastest. Read this as a result for these settings. It says nothing about tree models in general.

Higher AUC is better, lower log loss is better. The constant baseline predicts the train click rate (17.5%) for every impression and scores 0.4287 log loss. The whole search over C, final fit included, took about 68 minutes. All models trained on an M3 MacBook Air with 8 GB of RAM, DeepFM on the Apple GPU through PyTorch MPS.

Do the predicted rates match the clicks that happened?

Test impressions are sorted by predicted click chance and cut into ten equal groups. Each dot is one group: predicted rate on the horizontal axis, actual click rate on the vertical. A model that is calibrated sits on the dashed diagonal. Mostly the dots fall below it from the second group up, which is consistent with a test split that has a lower click rate than the training data.

What this does not show

This is 2014 mobile ad data

The data is mobile ad impressions from the 2014 Avazu Kaggle challenge. It is not Google Ads data, and nothing here describes how Google ranks or prices ads.

A random sample of the full set

Training used 7,998,480 impressions, validation 1,001,170, test 1,998,311, drawn at random from the reczoo/Avazu_x1 files. Models trained on more rows could rank differently.

Anonymised fields

The 22 fields are anonymised in the source data. I cannot tell what any column means, so the page says nothing about why a model ranks an impression high.

The click rate drifts between splits

Click rate is 17.5% in train, 16.8% in validation and 15.2% in test. That drift is real. I did not test its cause, and it is consistent with most calibration dots sitting below the diagonal from the second group up.

Logistic regression may be under-tuned

C was searched over 0.001, 0.003, 0.01 and the best value, 0.01, is the top of that grid. A larger C might score slightly higher, so the gap to DeepFM could be a little smaller than shown.

No money, no auction

Clicks are counted and never priced. The data has no bids, costs per click, conversions or budget pacing. The page also reports one run per model with no confidence intervals, and the headline model was picked by its test clicks at the 10% budget (it also has the best AUC and log loss).

Data and method

Data

  • reczoo/Avazu_x1 on Hugging Face, the processed copy used here
  • Avazu click-through rate prediction on Kaggle, the original 2014 challenge
  • DeepFM, Guo et al. 2017, the model architecture

Method

Each model scores every test impression. Sort by score, take the top k%, and count the clicks that actually happened in that slice. That count is the budget curve. Hyperparameters were chosen on the validation split, and the test split was used for scoring, with one exception: the headline model was picked by its test clicks (see what this does not show). Every number on this page is read from the results files at build time.

  • src/prepare_data.py samples the data
  • src/train_logreg.py, src/train_lgbm.py, src/train_deepfm.py
  • src/evaluate.py writes the summary
  • results/summary.json and results/logreg.json, the files behind the result figures
